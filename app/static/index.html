    When the network goes dark

    Bearing witness from above.

    When phone lines and internet are cut in a war zone, reports from the ground stop. Satellite and aerial imagery can still show what happened. This work follows humanitarian remote-sensing practice, such as that of the Humanitarian Research Lab at the Yale School of Public Health: document harm to civilians carefully, verify every lead, and hand evidence to the people who can act on it.

    My research focus In partnership with the Yale Humanitarian Research Lab

    Finding casualties during internet blackouts.

    This is my personal research. Through a partnership with the Yale Humanitarian Research Lab, I have helped with some of the lab's processes for analysing imagery from conflict zones.

    Convolutional networks, trained partly on simulated scenes, flag where aerial and satellite images may show casualties when no reports can get out. Simulation fills the gaps that real, labelled conflict imagery can't ethically or practically supply. The model surfaces leads for trained analysts. It does not count the dead.

    About the model

    How predictions are made.

    Architecture
    ResNet-50 pretrained on ImageNet, with a dropout and linear head for four classes.
    Training
    Five epochs on the head, then full fine-tuning at a lower learning rate.
    Explainability
    Grad-CAM on the final residual block highlights the regions that drove the decision.
    Limits
    Earthquake damage is visually subtle, and it is the class most often confused with the others.
